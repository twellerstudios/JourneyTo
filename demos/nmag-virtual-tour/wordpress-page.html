About the museum

Preserving our story, building our community.

Opened in 1892 as the Royal Victoria Institute, the museum has spent more than a century collecting, conserving and presenting Trinidad and Tobago’s human and natural heritage — from the nation’s earliest settlers to its Carnival arts and petroleum economy.

We are dedicated to the pillars of museum practice — research, collection, conservation, interpretation and exhibition — to educate, inspire and strengthen our community.

Featured artist

Michel-Jean Cazabon

1813 – 1888 · Naparima, Trinidad

Trinidad’s first internationally known painter. Cazabon trained in Paris under Paul Delaroche — having been sent to study medicine and quietly switched to fine art — then returned home and spent his career painting the island: the Caroni river, the Port of Spain docks, the estate houses and bamboo arches of the northern valleys.

Photography did not reach Trinidad in any quantity until after his death. Cazabon’s watercolours are, for much of the nineteenth century, the closest thing the country has to a photographic record of itself.

The museum holds a dedicated Cazabon gallery. Other works sit in private collections across Trinidad and abroad, and in the Louvre.

The building

A German Renaissance hall at the top of Frederick Street.

Designed by Daniel Meinerts Hahn, Chief Draughtsman of the Public Works Department — Dutch gables and a slate roof, built for the tropics and named for a queen’s jubilee.

1892

The Royal Victoria Institute opens

Opened on 17 September and named to mark Queen Victoria’s jubilee, the Institute is conceived as a combined museum, art gallery and technical school — a single civic building for the industrial and cultural instruction of the colony.

1920

Fire

The building is gutted. Much of the original holding is lost — the reason a museum founded in 1892 dates so much of its surviving collection to the decades after.

1923

Rebuilt on the same ground

Reconstruction restores the Institute to Frederick Street, opposite Memorial Park and just south of the Queen’s Park Savannah, where it has stood ever since.

1965

The National Museum and Art Gallery

Three years after independence, the Royal Victoria Institute is renamed. The collection is re-framed around a national story rather than an imperial one.

Today

Closed for renovation

The main building is currently closed to the public for restoration works — which is precisely the argument for a virtual tour. A digital floor keeps the collection open while the physical one is shut, and stays open to schools, the diaspora and researchers long after the scaffolding comes down.

  • The history is real. Dates, architecture, gallery themes, Cazabon, the Angostura puncheon, the branch museums and the visitor details are drawn from published sources.
  • The photography is real, but not all of it is the specific object. The building photo, both Cazabon paintings, and the museum’s emblem are the genuine article, sourced from Wikimedia Commons and the museum’s public Facebook page. The Pitch Lake, Angostura bottle, scarlet ibis, ocelot, steelband and Carnival photographs are real, correctly matched reference photography — not photographs of the individual pieces the museum holds.
  • The First Peoples image is representative. It shows a Caribbean zemi figure of the general period and region, not a piece from NMAG’s own archaeology collection.
  • In production, every photograph would be the museum’s own — the actual objects, on the actual shelf, shot on site.
